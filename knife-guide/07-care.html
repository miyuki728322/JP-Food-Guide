第七章 · Chapter Seven

Care, Rust & Storage

手入れと保管 — Teire to Hokan

A Japanese knife is not bought, it is kept. Five minutes of ritual a day is the entire price of a blade that outlives your career.

The Daily Ritual · 毎日の手入れ

Five Minutes That Decide Everything

Carbon steel (白紙, 青紙) begins to react with water within the hour. The routine below is non-negotiable for carbon and simply good practice for stainless.

一

During service · continuously

Wipe between tasks拭き取り

A damp side towel lives next to your board. After acid (citrus, tomato, onion) or protein, wipe the blade clean — both faces, spine to edge, edge away from your hand. A carbon blade left coated in lemon juice etches visibly in ten minutes.

二

End of service · immediately

Wash by hand, alone手洗い

Warm water, mild detergent, soft sponge, thirty seconds. Never in the sink with other tools — edges meet steel pans and chip. Never the dishwasher: the heat cycle dulls the edge, the detergent attacks the steel, and wa-handle wood cracks.

三

Within one minute of washing

Dry completely — every time完全乾燥

The single most important habit in this guide. A dry cloth over both faces, the spine, the handle, and especially the machi where blade meets handle — water pools there invisibly. 白紙 left damp overnight will greet you with orange rust at breakfast.

四

Carbon steel · before storage

A whisper of camellia oil椿油

Three drops of 椿油 (tsubaki oil) on a cloth, drawn once down each face. It is food-safe, flavourless, and forms the overnight barrier chromium would have provided. Going on holiday? Oil generously and wrap the blade in newspaper — the ink's compounds inhibit rust.

Why camellia? Pressed from the seeds of Camellia japonica, it is mostly oleic acid, a non-drying oil — so unlike linseed or olive oil it never turns sticky or rancid on the blade. It is thin enough to creep into the microscopic pores of the steel and seal out moisture and oxygen, and harmless if a trace reaches food. Swordsmiths have used it for centuries for the same reason; it is also kind to the wooden handle.

五

Weekly · ten minutes

Touch up the edge研ぎ直し

Ten light strokes per side on a #1000–3000 stone before the edge actually feels dull. Maintaining sharpness weekly is effortless; resurrecting a dead edge monthly is an hour of work. Chapter Six has the full method.

Never · 禁忌

Six Ways to Kill a Knife

✕

食洗機 — The dishwasher

Heat, detergent, and collision in one box. Voids most makers' warranties and ruins wa-handles outright.

✕

ガラス・大理石まな板 — Glass or stone boards

Harder than the blade. One session on glass undoes a sharpening. Wood (ideally hinoki) or soft composite only.

✕

冷凍食品 — Frozen food

At HRC 60+ the edge is hard but brittle — frozen blocks chip it like glass. Thaw first, or use a Western beater knife.

✕

骨・殻 — Bones and shells

Only the deba's heel is built for (fish) bone. Poultry and meat bones, crab shells, squash stems — wrong knife, chipped edge.

✕

こじる — Twisting and prying

Avocado pits, frozen lids, opening cans. A thin Japanese edge fails sideways — it cuts, it does not lever.

✕

濡れたまま放置 — Left wet

The quiet killer. Not abuse — neglect. A blade in a wet sink overnight loses more life than a year of service.

When It Happens Anyway · 錆びたら

Rust Triage

You will rust a carbon blade eventually. Everyone does. Speed is everything — surface rust is a five-minute fix; pitting is forever.

Surface bloom (orange dust): rub it away with a rust eraser (サビトール), a wine cork dipped in stone slurry, or a paste of baking soda. Work along the blade, never across the edge. Wash, dry, oil. Done.

Established spots (rough to the fingernail): raise mud on a #1000 stone and scrub the spots with the slurry and a cork. The spot will leave a pale scar in the patina — cosmetic only.

Pitting on the edge line: pits become chips. The edge must be sharpened back past the deepest pit on a coarse stone — or sent to a professional sharpener (研ぎ屋) if that is more steel than you've removed before.

Remember · 黒錆は味方

The blue-grey patina (黒錆) that builds on a working carbon blade is not damage — it is armor. Only orange-red rust (赤錆) is the enemy. Chapter Three shows the two side by side.

Storage · 保管

Where the Knife Sleeps

鞘 — Saya

Wooden sheath

A fitted ho-wood sheath, pinned shut. Protects the edge, wicks residual moisture, travels safely in a knife roll. Buy one fitted to each blade you own.

● The standard

包丁差し — Knife stand

Stand or in-drawer tray

Good when knives live at one station. Wood stands must stay dry inside — wash the slots' dust out monthly. In drawers, blades need a slotted tray; loose knives in a drawer are chipped knives.

● Excellent in place

マグネット — Magnetic strip

Wall magnet

Fast and visible — but place blades spine-first and peel them off edge-last, or the magnet itself micro-chips the edge. Wood-faced magnets only; bare steel strips are sandpaper.

● Fine, with technique

Long-term · 長期保管

For storage beyond a month: sharpen first (a clean edge resists rust better than a fatigued one), oil generously, wrap in newspaper, then in a dry cloth, and box it away from humidity. Check carbon blades monthly.

Kitchen Etiquette · 包丁の作法

The Unwritten Rules

In a Japanese kitchen the knife is personal property and a professional boundary. These rules are taught on day one — usually the hard way.

  • Never touch another chef's knife without explicit permission. A borrowed knife is re-sharpened to a stranger's angle — you are not borrowing a tool, you are erasing months of someone's edge work.
  • Pass handle-first, edge up and visible, announced ("knife!"). Set it down before the other person picks it up if you can — knives change hands on the board, not in the air.
  • The knife never points at a person on the board — edge faces you or the board's far rail when resting. Walking, blade rides point-down at your side, never on a cutting board carried flat.
  • Sharpening is done on your own time. The end-of-day stones are a ritual of reflection on the day's work — many itamae credit the habit for their consistency more than any recipe.
  • A dirty knife is a verdict. In traditional kitchens the head chef can read a cook's discipline from the state of their blade alone. The knife is your knife; its condition is your reputation.
"Show me your knife and I will tell you what kind of cook you are."
板前の言い伝え — itamae saying
