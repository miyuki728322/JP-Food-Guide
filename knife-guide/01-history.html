日本の包丁の歴史

The knife in your roll is the direct descendant of the samurai sword — same steel philosophy, same forging towns, often the same family names, six generations on.

The Long Arc · 千年の歩み

From Tamahagane to the Kitchen

Five moments explain everything about why Japanese knives are the way they are — including why the finest ones still rust.

c. 700s飛鳥・奈良

Iron arrives, the tatara burnsたたら製鉄

Japan's iron sand is poor ore, so smiths developed the たたら — a clay furnace fed with iron sand and charcoal for three sleepless days and nights. Its product, 玉鋼 (tamahagane, "jewel steel"), was scarce and uneven, which forced a uniquely Japanese habit: lamination. Precious hard steel for the edge only, cheap soft iron for the body — the kasumi construction in Chapter Three, born from scarcity.

794 – 1500s平安〜戦国

The age of the sword日本刀の時代

Eight centuries of warfare made Japanese swordsmiths the finest steelworkers on earth. Differential hardening — clay on the spine, bare steel at the edge, quenched in water — produced a blade hard enough to cut armor and springy enough not to snap. Every technique in a modern honyaki knife was perfected here, on weapons.

1574 – 1600s堺の台頭

Sakai cuts tobacco堺打刃物

When Portuguese traders brought tobacco, the port town of Sakai began forging the small, vicious knives that cut it — so well that the shogunate granted Sakai an official seal of quality and a national monopoly. The town's smiths split the craft in two: one master forges, another sharpens. That division of labour persists today and is why Sakai still makes some 90% of Japan's professional single-bevel knives.

1876廃刀令

The sword ban — the great pivot廃刀令

The Meiji government's 廃刀令 (Haitōrei) banned the wearing of swords. Overnight, hundreds of sword smiths faced extinction — and turned their craft to the kitchen. The katana's geometry became the yanagiba's; the sword polisher's stones became the sharpener's progression. When you draw a single-bevel blade through a block of tuna, you are using sword technology, repurposed.

1945 – now戦後〜現代

The santoku and the world三徳と世界へ

Postwar home kitchens wanted one knife for everything; the santoku — "three virtues" — was the answer, a hybrid of the vegetable nakiri and the Western gyuto. From the 1990s, Western chefs discovered Japanese steel, and towns like Seki, Takefu, and Sanjo built a global industry: traditional forging up front, modern metallurgy (VG-10, SG2) behind it.

The Source · 玉鋼

Three Days of Fire

たたら製鉄と玉鋼

A single tatara run consumed roughly thirteen tonnes of iron sand and as much charcoal to yield perhaps two and a half tonnes of usable steel — the smelters judging temperature entirely by the color of the flame. The best fragments, the tamahagane, went to sword smiths; today the last working tatara runs a few weeks each winter, almost exclusively for sword restoration.

Modern knife steel comes from Hitachi's Yasugi works — built in tatara country, in Shimane — which is why the paper steels of Chapter Three (白紙, 青紙) are considered the spiritual descendants of tamahagane: exceptionally pure, exceptionally keen, and exceptionally willing to rust.
