Steel

鋼材 — Kōzai

Every blade begins as a choice between four virtues that can never all be had at once. Understand the trade, and every knife catalogue in the world becomes legible.

The Honest Trade · 四つの徳

Four Virtues, Never All at Once

Forget chemistry for a moment. As a chef, a steel is exactly four questions: how sharp does it get, how long does it stay sharp, how easily can I bring it back, and will it rust on me?

Every steel on the market is a different answer to those four questions — and no steel answers yes to all of them. Harder steel holds an edge longer but takes longer on the stones. Pure carbon steel takes the keenest edge of all but will rust during a single busy service if you ignore it. Stainless forgives neglect but never feels quite as alive on the stone. There is no best steel. There is only the right steel for your station, your habits, and your patience.

切れ味

Kireaji · Sharpness

The keenness of the edge at its best — how cleanly it parts a tomato skin or a block of tuna without pressure.

永切れ

Nagagire · Edge retention

How many services that keenness survives. Driven mostly by hardness (HRC) and carbide content.

研ぎ易さ

Togiyasusa · Ease of sharpening

How quickly the stones raise a fresh edge. Soft, simple steels sharpen in minutes; hard powder steels make you earn it.

耐錆性

Taiseisei · Rust resistance

Chromium above ~13% makes a steel stainless. Below that, water and acid will find it — sometimes within the hour.

How to read HRC · 硬度

Rockwell hardness (HRC) is the number you will see on every spec sheet. German kitchen knives sit around 56–58. Japanese knives run 60–66. Harder steel holds a thinner, more acute edge for longer — but it is also more brittle: it chips rather than rolls, and it demands whetstones, never pull-through sharpeners.

Two Families · 鋼とステンレス

Carbon, Stainless, and the Space Between

Every steel in this chapter belongs to one of three camps.

炭素鋼 — Carbon steel (白紙, 青紙) is iron and carbon with almost nothing else. It takes the finest edge physics allows and sharpens like a dream, but it reacts with everything: water, lemon, onion, fish. It demands the daily ritual described in Chapter Seven.

ステンレス鋼 — Stainless steel (VG-10, モリブデン鋼, SG2) adds chromium to form a passive shield against rust. Modern Japanese stainless is superb — but the chromium carbides make the edge feel slightly "harder won" on the stones.

半ステンレス — The space between (銀三) is the chef's compromise: stainless chemistry tuned to behave like carbon on the stone. Many professional sushi chefs who once swore by 白紙 now carry 銀三 for exactly this reason.

霞 — Kasumi / 合わせ

Laminated construction · most knives

地金 Jigane — soft iron
鋼 Hagane — hard cutting steel

A thin plate of hard steel (the edge) is forge-welded to soft iron (the body). The soft jigane absorbs shock, makes sharpening faster, and lets the smith use expensive steel only where it cuts. The cloudy mist-like finish where the two metals meet gives kasumi its name.

本焼 — Honyaki

Single steel, differentially hardened · masterwork

鋼 Hagane — one solid piece

One bar of pure cutting steel, clay-coated and quenched like a katana so the spine stays springy while the edge turns glass-hard. Harder to forge, harder to sharpen, easy to crack in unskilled hands — and priced accordingly. A honyaki is a lifetime instrument, not a starter knife.

The Steels · 鋼材図鑑

A Working Chef's Steel Compendium

Six steels cover almost everything you will meet in a knife shop. For each: what it feels like to use, who it suits, and the care it demands.

炭素鋼 · Carbon

白紙

Shirogami · "White Paper Steel"

Hardness

60–64 HRC

The purest knife steel made — iron, carbon, and almost nothing else, named for the white paper Hitachi wraps the raw bars in. Because there are no stubborn carbides in the way, 白紙 takes the single keenest edge of any steel in this chapter, and it comes back to life on the stones faster than anything else. This is the steel of the traditional sushi counter: most fine 柳刃 and 薄刃 are 白紙二号 (No. 2); 白紙一号 (No. 1) carries more carbon — harder, keener, and a touch more brittle.

The price of that purity is fragility against the world: no chromium means no defense. Wipe-downs between tasks are not optional. Cut a lemon, walk away for ten minutes, and you will find a shadow etched on the blade.

手入れ · Care — strict

Rinse and dry completely after every use — a damp 白紙 blade left in the sink can show orange rust within the hour. Wipe with a dry cloth between ingredients during service. Before any storage longer than overnight, coat lightly with 椿油 (camellia oil). Never leave it wet, never leave it dirty, never put it in a dishwasher.

炭素鋼 · Carbon

青紙

Aogami · "Blue Paper Steel"

Hardness

61–65 HRC

Take 白紙, add a measure of tungsten and chromium, and you get 青紙 — wrapped in blue paper. The additions form tough carbides that let the edge survive far longer between sharpenings, at the cost of a little of 白紙's effortless feel on the stone. 青紙二号 is the workhorse of high-end gyuto; 青紙一号 trades more toughness for keenness.

青紙スーパー (Aogami Super) pushes everything further — more carbon, more tungsten, added vanadium and molybdenum — reaching HRC 63–65 with edge retention that approaches powder steels while still sharpening like a carbon steel. For many professionals it is the finest all-round carbon steel made.

手入れ · Care — strict

Marginally more forgiving than 白紙 thanks to its trace chromium, but treat it identically: wash, dry every time, oil before storage. The tungsten carbides also mean chips are harder to grind out — avoid bones, frozen food, and glass boards absolutely.

半ステンレス気質 · Stainless

銀三

Ginsan / Gin-3 · "Silver Paper No. 3"

Hardness

60–62 HRC

Hitachi's answer to a famous question: can a stainless steel feel like carbon? 銀三 keeps the fine, simple grain of the paper steels but carries just enough chromium (≈13–14%) to shrug off rust. On the stones it is the closest thing to 白紙 in the stainless world — fast to raise an edge, with that same clean, glassy bite.

It has become the modern sushi chef's steel: traditional single-bevel knives in 銀三 deliver 90% of the carbon experience with none of the 3 a.m. rust anxiety. If you love the idea of carbon but work a brutal, wet station, start here.

手入れ · Care — relaxed

Wash and dry like any good knife. It can sit damp through a service without drama, but salt and citrus left overnight can still pit the edge. Dry before storage and it will stay flawless for years.

ステンレス鋼 · Stainless

VG-10

V-Gold 10 · Takefu Steel

Hardness

60–61 HRC

The most widespread premium stainless in Japanese knives, made by Takefu in Echizen. Cobalt and vanadium let VG-10 hold a serious edge at a true 60+ HRC while staying fully stainless — this is the steel inside most of the famous Damascus-clad gyuto sold worldwide.

In hand it is the dependable professional: sharp enough for fine work, tough enough for the line, indifferent to a wet board. Its one quirk — the edge can develop a fine, toothy burr on the stones that takes practice to deburr cleanly. Strop carefully and it rewards you.

手入れ · Care — easy

Normal professional hygiene is enough: wash, dry, store. Still no dishwasher — the heat cycles and detergent dull and discolor any fine edge — and still whetstones only.

粉末鋼 · Powder stainless

SG2 / R2

Super Gold 2 · powder metallurgy

Hardness

62–64 HRC

Powder metallurgy atomizes molten steel into a fine dust, then presses it back together — producing a grain so uniform that very high hardness arrives without the brittleness it would normally bring. SG2 holds a working edge two to three times longer than VG-10 and laughs at rust.

The trade: sharpening sessions are slower and demand quality stones (diamond or hard ceramic helps), and the price is the highest of any steel here. For a chef who wants maximum time cooking and minimum time at the stones, it is worth every yen.

手入れ · Care — easy, but…

Rust care is trivial — but at HRC 63 the edge is unforgiving of abuse. No twisting, no bones, no frozen food, soft cutting boards only. A chip in SG2 means a long, expensive repair session.

ステンレス鋼 · Stainless

モリブデン鋼

Molybdenum steel · AUS-8, MV steel

Hardness

56–59 HRC

The honest workhorse — the molybdenum-vanadium stainless family (AUS-8 and kin) found in culinary-school knives, staff knives, and the prep kitchens of half the restaurants in Japan. It will not hold a 白紙 edge and was never meant to: it is soft enough to sharpen in five minutes on any stone, tough enough to survive being borrowed, and completely rustproof in daily use.

Do not dismiss it. A モリブデン gyuto touched up weekly outcuts a neglected premium knife every single time — and for staff training (Chapter Five) it is exactly what you want under inexperienced hands.

手入れ · Care — minimal

Wash, dry, done. The only discipline it demands is frequent touch-ups: the edge fades gently rather than staying crisp, so a weekly visit to a #1000 stone keeps it honest.

Rule of thumb · 目安

Love sharpening → 白紙. Want the best carbon all-rounder → 青紙スーパー. Want carbon feel without rust → 銀三. Want to never think about it → VG-10. Hate sharpening → SG2. Equipping a whole kitchen → モリブデン鋼.

Know the Difference · 錆と黒錆

Patina Is Not Rust

New carbon-steel owners panic at the first discoloration. Don't. There are two oxides, and one of them is on your side.

黒錆 — Patina

Protective · leave it

A stable blue-grey-to-charcoal film that forms as carbon steel meets food acids. It actually shields the blade from red rust. Most carbon users deliberately build one by cutting onions or wiping the blade with mustard. A patinated blade is a used, loved blade.

赤錆 — Red rust

Destructive · remove now

Orange, powdery, and it digs pits into the steel — on the edge, pits become chips. Caused by water left on the blade. Remove early with a rust eraser (サビトール) or #1000 stone mud, then re-oil. Deep pitting near the edge may need professional regrinding.
