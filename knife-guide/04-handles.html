第四章 · Chapter Four

Handles

柄 — E

The handle decides where the knife balances, how it steers, and how your hand feels at hour ten of a twelve-hour shift. East and West answer the question differently.

Two Philosophies · 和柄と洋柄

Wa and Yō

Every Japanese knife ships one of two ways: the traditional wooden wa-handle, or the Western riveted yō-handle. Neither is better. They balance differently, age differently, and suit different hands.

和柄 — Wa

Traditional Japanese

A light wooden grip friction-fitted over a hidden tang that is burned in, not riveted. The lightness shifts the balance point forward onto the blade — the knife feels eager, blade-heavy, alive at the tip. Preferred for single-bevel knives and precision work.

Because the fit is friction, a wa-handle is a consumable: any knife shop swaps one in minutes. A century-old blade on its fifth handle is normal and honorable.

BalanceForward — on the blade
WeightVery light
CareKeep dry; oil occasionally; never dishwasher
RepairReplaceable in minutes, cheaply

洋柄 — Yō

Western style

A full tang sandwiched between riveted scales of pakkawood or micarta — the construction of a German chef's knife carrying a Japanese blade. The extra rear weight moves balance back to the pinch point, giving a planted, durable, familiar feel for cooks trained on Western knives.

Effectively waterproof and indestructible on the line, at the cost of nimbleness — and when it finally does wear out, it cannot be replaced, only re-scaled by a specialist.

BalanceNeutral to rear — at the pinch
WeightModerate to heavy
CareNearly none — wipe and go
RepairSpecialist job; rarely worth it

Cross-sections · 柄の形状

The Shape in Your Palm

Wa-handles come in four classic cross-sections. The differences sound small and feel enormous by the end of a prep list.

小判型

Oval

The default. Comfortable in every grip, no hot spots — but gives the hand no index of where the edge points.

八角型

Octagonal

The professional favourite. Facets lock the blade's orientation into your palm — you feel the edge's direction blind.

栗型

Chestnut / D-shape

A ridge nests into the fingers — superbly secure, but handed: a right-hand D fights a left hand.

角型

Squared

Seen on heavy deba — maximum purchase for wet, forceful work where the grip must not roll.

Materials · 柄の素材

Woods and the Ferrule

The collar where handle meets blade is the 桂 (katsura, ferrule) — traditionally water-buffalo horn, dense enough to seal the joint against water, the handle's one weak point.

朴 — HōMagnolia · the standard

Feather-light, faintly antibacterial, and cheap to replace — the white wood on 90% of wa-handles. It darkens and wears with use; replacing it every few years is part of the knife's life.

栗 — KuriChestnut · workmanlike

Harder and more water-tolerant than hō, with visible grain. Common on Tosa kurouchi knives and rustic single-bevels.

黒檀 — KokutanEbony · the upgrade

Dense, near-black, takes a glassy polish and shrugs off water. Adds weight that shifts balance slightly rearward — some prefer exactly that. The classic pairing with a buffalo-horn ferrule on presentation-grade knives.

紫檀 — ShitanRosewood · the middle path

Warm-toned, durable, moderately priced — the most common step up from hō wood on mid-range knives.

Fit check · 目安

Whatever the wood: the ferrule must sit flush with no gap at the blade (water enters there), the handle should reach to the heel of your palm in a pinch grip, and the knife should balance within a finger's width of the place you actually hold it.
